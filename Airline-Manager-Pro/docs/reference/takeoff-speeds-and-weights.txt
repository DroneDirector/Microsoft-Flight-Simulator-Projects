COMMERCIAL AIRCRAFT TAKEOFF SPEEDS, WEIGHTS AND PASSENGER LOADS
Reference guide | Prepared 29 October 2026

IMPORTANT SCOPE AND SAFETY NOTE This is a broad reference guide to major
commercial passenger-jet families and selected regional aircraft. It is
not literally every aircraft ever built or every sub-variant.
Manufacturer weight options, engine options, certification changes,
airline cabin layouts and aircraft modifications create many variants.

Takeoff speeds below are broad, approximate educational ranges in
indicated airspeed, not guaranteed values for a particular flight. For
many commercial aircraft, manufacturers do not publish one universal
takeoff speed because crews calculate V1, VR and V2 for each departure
using the approved performance system/manual. Actual speeds can be
outside these ranges. Do not use this document to operate an aircraft or
calculate takeoff performance.

MAXIMUM TAKEOFF WEIGHT (MTOW) MTOW is the maximum permitted aircraft
weight at the start of the takeoff run for a particular approved weight
variant. It is not the weight every flight must reach, nor the minimum
weight needed to take off. Exact MTOW differs by sub-variant, engine,
certification and airline configuration. Figures below are approximate
representative published values or family-level ranges; check current
manufacturer documentation for the exact aircraft.

PASSENGER WEIGHT EXAMPLES There is no single required passenger count or
passenger weight for takeoff. An aircraft can depart with few passengers
if the total aircraft weight, balance, fuel, runway and performance
requirements are satisfied. Conversely, a full passenger cabin does not
guarantee the aircraft can depart with maximum fuel and cargo.

For rough comparison only, this guide uses 100 kg per passenger as an
illustrative combined passenger-plus-baggage allowance. This is NOT a
universal regulatory standard: actual operator mass assumptions and
actual baggage/cargo weights vary. The passenger-load figure is simply:
illustrative passenger payload = passenger count x 100 kg It does not
include the aircraft itself, fuel, crew, catering, cargo or other
operational items, and must not be mistaken for a takeoff-weight
calculation.

SPEED UNITS 1 knot (kt) = 1.852 km/h. Takeoff speed means airspeed, not
speed over the runway. Headwind can reduce groundspeed at a given
airspeed; tailwind can increase groundspeed and runway requirements.

HOW TO READ THE LIST • Typical seats = broad common airline layout, not
a certified limit for every operator. • Approx. takeoff speed = broad
illustrative range for the family/model, not a specific V1/VR/V2. • MTOW
= approximate maximum takeoff weight, metric tonnes unless stated
otherwise. • Passenger payload examples show the 100 kg/person
illustration at the low and high ends of the typical seat range. • “Jet”
sections cover passenger jets; a final section includes commonly used
commercial turboprops for comparison.

8. WHAT WEIGHT DOES AN AIRCRAFT “NEED” TO TAKE OFF?
========================================================================

There is no single minimum takeoff weight that applies to a model. A
flight’s takeoff weight is broadly made up of: operating empty weight +
crew + passengers + baggage/cargo + usable fuel The precise definitions
and accounting categories depend on the operator’s weight-and-balance
system.

A flight must be within the applicable limits, including: • Maximum
takeoff weight (MTOW) • Maximum landing weight (MLW), considering
expected landing weight • Maximum zero-fuel weight (MZFW), where
applicable • Centre-of-gravity and trim limits • Runway length and
runway condition • Temperature, pressure altitude, wind and obstacles •
Engine/thrust and flap configuration • Required takeoff performance and
regulatory safety margins

A jet may be too heavy to depart from a short runway even if it is below
MTOW. Conversely, it may depart well below MTOW when lightly loaded. A
maximum passenger count does not determine whether it can take off; the
full aircraft weight and performance calculation do.

9. WHY TAKEOFF SPEED CHANGES
========================================================================

Weight: For otherwise similar conditions and configuration, the speed
needed to generate a given lift increases approximately with the square
root of weight. A 20% weight increase corresponds to about a 9.5%
increase in the idealized speed for the same lift, all else equal. This
is a simplified aerodynamic relationship, not a method for calculating
real takeoff speeds.

Wind: A headwind usually reduces groundspeed at the required airspeed. A
tailwind usually raises groundspeed and increases takeoff distance.
Crosswind limits also apply.

Air density: Hot weather and high-altitude airports reduce air density
and can reduce engine performance. This often increases runway distance
and may affect performance-limited weight. Indicated airspeed does not
simply increase in proportion to temperature or altitude.

Flaps and aircraft design: Takeoff flap settings and wing design change
lift characteristics. Different variants can have different certified
speeds even at similar weights.

Runway and obstacles: Wet/contaminated pavement, slope, runway length
and obstacles affect the permitted takeoff weight and performance
calculations.

========================================================================
10. TAKEOFF SPEED TERMS
========================================================================

V1: The takeoff decision speed used in the planned stop/continue
decision. It is not simply “the speed where the plane must always take
off.” VR: Rotation speed, at which the pilot initiates the pitch-up for
takeoff. V2: Takeoff safety speed used for the initial climb performance
target. Liftoff speed: The speed at which the aircraft actually leaves
the runway; it is related to but not necessarily identical to VR.

The crew calculates these speeds for the specific aircraft, weight,
runway, weather and configuration. Do not infer a flight’s actual
V1/VR/V2 from the broad ranges in this guide.
