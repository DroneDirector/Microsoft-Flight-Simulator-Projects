COMMERCIAL AIRCRAFT LANDING SPEEDS, WEIGHTS AND PASSENGER LOADS
Reference guide | Prepared 29 October 2026

IMPORTANT SCOPE AND SAFETY NOTE This guide follows the same broad
aircraft families as the companion takeoff-speed guide. It covers major
commercial passenger jets and selected regional turboprops. It is a
broad reference, not literally every aircraft ever built or every
sub-variant.

Landing speeds below are approximate educational ranges for final
approach / threshold airspeed in landing configuration. They are not
guaranteed landing speeds for a specific flight. The actual target
approach speed is calculated for the aircraft’s actual landing weight,
flap/configuration, wind, and operating procedures. Never use this guide
to operate an aircraft or determine whether a particular runway is long
enough.

KEY TERMS • Approach speed (often called VAPP): the target speed used on
final approach. It is usually higher than the aircraft’s reference speed
to account for wind and other factors. • Threshold speed: airspeed as
the aircraft crosses the runway threshold; published
aircraft-characteristics tables may state a final approach speed under
specified conditions. • Touchdown speed: speed over the runway at wheel
contact. It is lower than threshold speed because the aircraft
decelerates during the flare, but it varies. • Maximum landing weight
(MLW): the maximum permitted aircraft weight for landing. It is not the
weight every flight must have, nor the minimum weight needed to land. •
Maximum takeoff weight (MTOW): a separate limit; it is usually higher
than MLW. A flight may depart heavy and burn fuel before landing.

UNITS 1 knot (kt) = 1.852 km/h. Landing speed is airspeed, not
groundspeed. A headwind means lower groundspeed for the same airspeed; a
tailwind means higher groundspeed and generally increases landing
distance.

HOW TO READ THE LIST • Typical seats are common airline layouts, not a
certified limit for every operator. • Approx. landing approach speed is
a broad illustrative range in knots and km/h, not an operational speed
for a specific flight. • Approx. MLW is a representative family-level
figure in metric tonnes. Weight options vary by exact model, engine,
certification and operator. • Passenger count does not set a landing
speed by itself. Actual landing weight includes the aircraft, crew,
passengers, baggage/cargo and remaining fuel.

7. HOW PASSENGER LOAD AFFECTS LANDING WEIGHT AND SPEED
========================================================================

Passenger count alone does not determine landing speed. The aircraft’s
total landing weight includes: operating aircraft weight + crew +
passengers + baggage/cargo + remaining fuel

For otherwise similar configuration and conditions, higher landing
weight generally requires a higher reference/approach speed and
increases landing energy and stopping distance. The exact change must be
calculated from approved aircraft performance data; do not estimate an
actual landing speed from passenger count.

8. MAXIMUM LANDING WEIGHT VS. MAXIMUM TAKEOFF WEIGHT
========================================================================

MLW is not MTOW. Many airliners have a maximum landing weight
substantially lower than their maximum takeoff weight because landing
imposes different structural and performance limits.

An aircraft may take off at a high weight, burn fuel in flight, then
land below MLW. If it must land above MLW, the crew follows the
aircraft’s approved procedures and company guidance; this document is
not a guide for such a decision.

A legal, safe landing also depends on: • Actual landing weight and
centre of gravity • Runway length, slope, surface and braking action •
Wind, gusts, crosswind and weather • Temperature, pressure altitude and
runway contamination • Flap/gear configuration and aircraft system
status • Approach stability and required regulatory safety margins •
Available stopping distance and approved landing-performance
calculations

Being below MLW does not by itself prove that a landing is safe on a
given runway.

========================================================================
9. HOW WIND, SPEED AND RUNWAY CONDITIONS AFFECT LANDING
========================================================================

Headwind: Usually reduces groundspeed for a given airspeed and can
reduce landing distance, although gusts and wind changes require careful
speed management. Tailwind: Usually increases groundspeed and landing
distance; tailwind limits and performance calculations apply. Wet or
contaminated runway: Can reduce braking effectiveness and increase
stopping distance. Higher landing weight: Generally increases approach
speed and kinetic energy, making stopping performance more demanding.
Excessive approach speed: Can increase float and touchdown distance,
potentially increasing runway-overrun risk. Too little airspeed: Can
reduce stall margin and increase the risk of an unstable approach or
hard landing. Temperature and altitude: Affect aircraft performance and
runway margins; they are included in approved landing calculations.

Approach speed is managed by pilots throughout descent, approach, flare
and touchdown. Touchdown speed is not one fixed number because it
depends on approach speed, wind, flare technique and conditions.
